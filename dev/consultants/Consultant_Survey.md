## Invitation and consent

You are invited to take part in a survey of intensive care physicians about how sepsis that develops *during* an ICU stay is recognised today, and what you would expect from a system that warns of it a few hours in advance.

The survey is part of a doctoral research project that is building and testing such a system on de-identified ICU records from the United States (one academic medical centre and about two hundred community and teaching hospitals). Your answers will do two things: describe current practice, and set several clinical choices in the study design that the records themselves cannot settle, such as how many alerts per day a unit can accept.

- The survey takes about 20 minutes.
- Taking part is voluntary. You may stop at any time and skip any question.
- Your answers are anonymous. We do not record your name, and the characteristics we ask about (role, years of experience, unit type) are reported only in groups.
- Free-text answers may be quoted anonymously in publications.

## Section D. Judging whether a patient is getting worse after sepsis has started

To study which patients deteriorate after sepsis is recognised, we need a written rule that reads the chart at a fixed time after onset. Your answers decide that rule.

**D1.** In practice, when you review a septic ICU patient one or two days after treatment started, which signs tell you most reliably that they are getting *worse*? Tick up to three.

- [ ] Rising vasopressor requirement
- [ ] Rising lactate or failure of lactate to clear
- [ ] Worsening oxygenation or need for more ventilatory support
- [ ] Falling urine output or rising creatinine
- [ ] Falling platelets or worsening coagulation
- [ ] Rising bilirubin
- [ ] Worsening consciousness
- [ ] Persistent fever or new fever
- [ ] Rising or persistently high SOFA score
- [ ] My overall impression at the bedside
- [ ] Other: ______________________

**D2.** *Our proposed rule.* At 48 hours after onset a patient counts as **deteriorating** if any of the following holds:

1. death in hospital;
2. a rise of at least 2 points in the SOFA score, comparing the same organ components at onset and at 48 hours;
3. an increase in vasopressor dose of at least 0.1 micrograms per kilogram per minute (noradrenaline-equivalent) above the dose at onset.

A patient counts as **improving** if alive at discharge with no such rise and no vasopressor at 48 hours. A patient who leaves the ICU alive before 48 hours counts as improving and is reported separately. We will also read the chart at 72 hours as a check; in our records the two readings agreed in 98 of 100 patients.

**E2.** Roughly how many *clinically irrelevant* alerts does a nurse on your unit deal with per patient per shift? ______

**E3.** *What we found.* An alert level that is tuned in one hospital can land in the wrong place in another: when we tested our system in a second hospital with a level fixed in the first, it sometimes fell silent, and published experience with a widely used commercial sepsis alert found that it fired on about one in five of all inpatients. There are two ways to set the level:

- **A fixed alert rate.** The system is set to alert on a fixed share of patient-hours, for example 2 % of all hours on the unit. The number of alerts per day stays the same wherever it is used; the price is that it does not rise or fall when sepsis becomes more or less common.
- **A fixed risk level.** The system alerts whenever the estimated likelihood of sepsis crosses a set value, for example 20 %. The alert rate then follows the case mix and can be very different between hospitals.

## Appendix for the research team (not shown to respondents)

### How the answers feed the study protocol

The protocol (Section 10c.2 item 12 and Section 11) names three clinical choices that the data cannot settle. Each is read from specific items; the rest of the instrument describes practice and expectations and stands on its own as a survey study.

| Protocol choice | Items | Proposed position | Rule for adopting a change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Credit for warnings after the first antibiotic | C3, C4 | no credit in the primary presentation; lead time against the antibiotic reported | if a majority choose a use for post-antibiotic warnings in C3 and more than half disagree with the approach in C4, the primary presentation adds a second column crediting warnings up to the formal onset |
| Course definition | D2 to D6 | 48 h primary, 72 h sensitivity; death, paired SOFA rise of 2, vasopressor escalation of 0.1; deaths under treatment limitation count as deterioration | the modal answer to each of D3, D4, D5 and D6 replaces the proposed value if it is chosen by more than half of respondents |
| Alert level and alert budget | E3, E4, E5, E6, E7 | fixed alert rate; budget 0.5 false alerts per patient-day (5 per 10 patients per day); same across unit types; two consecutive hours | E3: the modal answer; E4: the median acceptable burden becomes the false-alarm budget (expressed per patient-day); E6: a unit-specific budget is adopted only if more than half choose "different"; E7: the modal persistence |

The acceptable-burden question E4 is deliberately anchored at the development result (about 1 false alert per 3 patient-days at half the episodes caught, median warning 4 hours), with E5 testing the trade-off against sensitivity. A budget tighter than the current operating point moves the threshold, not the model.

### Analysis plan for the survey as a publication

- **Design:** cross-sectional, anonymous, self-administered questionnaire; convenience sample of intensive care physicians at the participating hospitals, extended by invitation through national intensive care societies if the investigators choose.
- **Sample:** all consultants and senior registrars of the participating units are invited; a minimum of 30 complete responses is set for the descriptive report, with the response rate recorded.
- **Quantitative items:** counts and percentages for categorical items; medians with interquartile ranges for numeric and Likert items; comparisons between unit types (medical, surgical, general) and between experience bands (under 10 years, 10 years or more) by Fisher's exact test or the Mann-Whitney test, reported as exploratory.
- **Qualitative items (B10, B11, C8, E9, F6, F7, G1):** inductive thematic analysis by two readers, with a coding frame agreed after the first ten responses and disagreements resolved by discussion; themes reported with anonymised illustrative quotations.
- **Reporting:** CHERRIES for web-based surveys or the CROSS checklist for self-administered questionnaires; the instrument is published as supplementary material.
- **Ethics:** an exemption or expedited review for an anonymous professional survey is sought before distribution; the approval reference is entered in the invitation.

### Wording conventions used

Clinical terms only: "warning" or "alert" rather than "alarm" or "prediction"; "estimated likelihood" rather than "probability output"; "fixed alert rate" rather than "quantile operating point"; "level" rather than "threshold"; "noradrenaline-equivalent dose" rather than "norepinephrine-equivalent"; "tested in a second hospital" rather than "transfer"; "re-set on local patients" rather than "recalibration"; "two consecutive hours" rather than "persistence". The SOFA score is named in full once (Sequential Organ Failure Assessment) and treated as known thereafter.
